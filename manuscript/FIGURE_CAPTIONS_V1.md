# Figure captions — Louisiana hydrological-buffering manuscript

## Figure 1. Matched local availability turns habitat selection into a temporal environmental-buffering test

**(A)** Repeated King Rail homing events were paired with nearby random microhabitat plots sampled within the same short time window. After source-defined quality control, the analysis retained **190 matched events from 10 individual birds**.  
**(B)** Conventional habitat selection compares used and available states at a single observation time. Here, the response is instead the temporal trajectory of water depth experienced at repeatedly used points, evaluated against pseudo-trajectories drawn from the local availability set at the same observation times. The individual bird is the biological replication unit.

## Figure 2. Individual King Rails experienced more stable water-depth trajectories than matched local availability

**(A)** State Retention Index (SRI) for each of 10 birds, where positive values indicate that temporal variance in used water depth was lower than the median variance of event-matched random pseudo-trajectories. All **10/10** birds were positive; median SRI was **0.886** (range **0.402–0.976**; one-sided sign test *p* = **0.00098**).  
**(B)** Time-ordered retention based on mean absolute successive changes in water depth. All **10/10** birds again showed positive retention, with median **0.658** (range **0.388–0.879**; sign test *p* = **0.00098**). The panels show individual birds rather than treating repeated events as independent biological replicates.

## Figure 3. Realised microhabitat use weakly transmitted temporal variation in local water depth

**(A)** Pooled within-bird slope of used water depth on mean event-matched available water depth. The observed slope was **0.162**, compared with a matched pseudo-used null median of **1.001**.  
**(B)** Coupling reduction relative to the pseudo-used null across the primary analysis (**83.8%**), a sensitivity excluding dry random plots (**85.5%**), and a sensitivity requiring all retained used and random points to be flooded (**82.0%**). These percentages quantify reduction in the used-on-available water-depth slope; they are not percentages of physiological stress removed.  
**(C)** Individual observed slopes relative to each bird's pseudo-used null median. All **10/10** birds had observed slopes below their own null median. Because geographic coordinates cannot be linked unambiguously to the matched microhabitat events, the figure supports environmental buffering by realised habitat use, not a measured displacement-mediated causal mechanism.

## Figure 4. Hydrological buffering is strong but finite across individuals

For each bird, extreme local hydrological conditions were defined independently as the top quartile of deviation in event-matched available water depth from that bird's typical available state. Extreme-event retention is plotted against the corresponding within-bird availability-mismatch scale. **Nine of ten** birds retained positive buffering under these conditions; median extreme retention was **0.629**, while one bird showed negative retention. The analysis demonstrates heterogeneity and finite buffering capacity; it does not identify a universal hydrological threshold for broad relocation.

## Figure 5. Predicted transition from local buffering to broader relocation

Conceptual ecological boundary linking the Lake Erie result to independent King Rail context. When suitable hydrological states remain reachable locally, repeated microhabitat use can buffer the environmental trajectory experienced within a resident home range. When local compensation fails, broader relocation is predicted to become more likely. In an independent South Carolina King Rail study, **5/9** birds with breeding and non-breeding telemetry shifted seasonal home ranges, with a mean shift of approximately **2.9 km** (range **0.7–7.5 km**) under changing wetland conditions and management. This is external biological context, not replication of the Lake Erie SRI or coupling estimands.
