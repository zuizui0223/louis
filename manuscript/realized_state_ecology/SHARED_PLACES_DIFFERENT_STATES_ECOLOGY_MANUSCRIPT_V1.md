## Abstract

Animals sharing a geographical location need not occupy the same ecological state. Their exposure to habitat conditions also depends on when they use a place, which vertical or microhabitat states they occupy, and whether they repeatedly maintain or switch those states as local opportunities change. We examined these three aspects of realized habitat use across four complementary vertebrate observation programmes, using audited results from the ODSP, batter and Louisiana research repositories. In Snapshot Serengeti, camera detections from 17 mammal species exhibited within-site temporal differentiation (conditional species–time information = 0.224 nats, permutation p = 0.005), with positive held-out predictive gains in all three site groups. Among six bat tracking panels representing four taxa, five retained repeatable individual differences in the shape of vertical use after coarse horizontal occupancy and each session's median altitude were controlled; the motivating *Tadarida teniotis* panel was an explicit exception. At Lake Erie, 10 of 10 King Rails (*Rallus elegans*) used water depths that fluctuated less than matched local available depths (median individual state-retention index = 0.886; 83.8% lower coupling to locally available depth than matched pseudo-use). A distinct California waterfowl programme showed excess successive use of the same habitat category beyond time-matched availability and fixed individual preference in four species; after controlling source season and photoperiod, scarcity of the previously used category was associated with switching in two goose species and northern pintail, but not convincingly in mallard. A further within-bird exploratory analysis revealed a **lesser-snow-goose-specific day/night boundary excess in habitat-category switching** (+8.47 percentage points, 95% candidate-individual interval +5.72 to +11.16). A previous observed Protected-category destination was followed by a Rice-category destination more often near nightfall than the reverse diel boundary, even after time-matched available-habitat and individual preference references, in lesser snow geese (+32.36 points, +22.13 to +43.33) and northern pintails (+55.54, +37.53 to +72.77); the analogous effects in greater white-fronted geese were uncertain and mallards were insufficiently replicated. These directed sequences do not establish feeding behaviour or movement routes. Together these observations support a comparative ecological picture in which shared sites contain biologically organized differences in timing, vertical use and realized habitat-state trajectories. The outcomes are not the same estimand, and they do not establish one common adaptive mechanism. They instead show why the ecological consequences of habitat overlap depend on *who uses which state and when*, not only on mapped presence.

**Keywords:** realized niche; individual specialization; temporal partitioning; three-dimensional habitat use; environmental exposure; microhabitat fidelity; waterfowl; habitat heterogeneity.

## Introduction

Habitat overlap is usually inferred from animals occupying the same landscape cell, home range or broad vegetation type. Such overlap can mask biologically meaningful variation. The same forest canopy and airspace may be used at different heights; mammals visiting the same camera site may be detected at different times; and animals exposed to fluctuating wetland water levels can repeatedly select a comparatively narrow range of microhabitat depths. The geographical footprint of a population is therefore not equivalent to the sequence of ecological conditions experienced by its members.

The ecology of individual specialization has long challenged the assumption that conspecific individuals are interchangeable consumers of a pooled resource spectrum (Bolnick et al. 2003). Work on three-dimensional niches further emphasizes that horizontal range overlap need not imply shared vertical use (Gámez and Harris 2022). Movement ecology provides a natural link between habitat availability and experienced conditions, because the states animals encounter depend jointly on environmental change and their own habitat use (Nathan et al. 2008). However, these ideas suggest at least three distinguishable ecological questions: **who uses a site at which times; whether individuals repeatedly use different states within the same spatial setting; and whether their actual exposure remains stable despite changes in locally available conditions**.

A particularly important distinction concerns persistence. An animal may repeatedly encounter similar conditions because it is resident in an environmentally stable patch, because it switches among patches offering similar conditions, or simply because most available patches share the same state. Conversely, an individual may possess a repeatable state-use profile without experiencing a stable environment if availability changes or use shifts over time. These ecological possibilities produce different implications for habitat management, competitive overlap and exposure to environmental fluctuations.

Here we bring together four deliberately different, independently developed case-study programmes: (1) species-specific detection times within Serengeti camera sites; (2) individual vertical use in bats; (3) water-depth use by resident King Rails in Lake Erie marshes; and (4) categorical habitat trajectories in wintering waterfowl in California. They represent ecological contrasts across time, three-dimensional space and repeated individual habitat choice. They were not originally sampled under one common protocol, so we do not force their effects onto a common numerical scale.

We ask whether three ecological predictions have support within their appropriate study systems. **First**, co-occurrence at a site should conceal repeatable organization of additional states, such as time of detection or use of vertical strata. **Second**, individuals should sometimes remain non-exchangeable in their state-use profiles even after broad horizontal occupancy is accounted for. **Third**, realized environmental-state sequences should sometimes be more persistent than current local opportunity or average habitat preference alone predicts, while state switching should respond to changes in the available choice set. The synthesis aims to identify the biological patterns, their differences among taxa and the alternative ecological explanations still open, rather than introduce a new prediction algorithm.

## Materials and methods

### Comparative evidence and source systems

We assembled ecological outcomes from three existing, independently versioned open research repositories. Each result was checked against its original reported numerical summary and analytical unit, and the exact source-file provenance is retained in the accompanying evidence snapshot. Selection of these examples occurred after the original studies had reported their outcomes. Consequently, this is a **post-result comparative ecological synthesis**, not an independent prospective test of a universal vertebrate hypothesis.

**Temporal differentiation at shared savanna sites.** The Snapshot Serengeti survey (Swanson et al. 2015) provided site-labelled camera detections. The ODSP secondary analysis retained 17 admitted mammal species and evaluated whether species identity carried information about recorded detection-time categories after conditioning on camera site. The result includes a within-site association and three held-out site-group tests. Camera detections are not identical to true continuous activity, and the analysis did not experimentally manipulate competition or detection probabilities.

**Individual vertical-use distributions in bats.** The batter comparative analysis used six public tracking panels from four bat taxa: *Tadarida teniotis*, *Eidolon helvum*, *Hypsignathus monstrosus* and three *Phyllostomus hastatus* panel years. Its biological question was whether a bat's own previous sessions predicted the distributional shape of subsequently used vertical states better than other individuals' sessions, when compared under common coarse horizontal occupancy. Session-median centring removed absolute altitude differences and any constant additive device offset. The focal *Tadarida* observations partly overlap ODSP's earlier vertical-niche analysis and are not counted as an independent ecological replication.

**Experienced wetland depth in resident rails.** The King Rail archive (Brewer et al. 2023; Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.6604660) contains used microhabitat water depths and contemporaneous randomly sampled local depths. The Louisiana analysis evaluated how strongly each bird's realized water-depth series varied relative to sequences sampled from those available alternatives, with the individual bird as replication unit. Ten source bird identities contributed 190 matched events. A secondary association tested whether heterogeneity among the local alternatives was compatible with smaller deviations in the depth actually used. The archive does not supply a verified event-by-event link between water-depth events and geographical displacement.

**Temporal habitat use in wintering waterfowl.** The independent California programme of Overton and Casazza (2023; USGS 10.5066/P9ELSUHN) supplied time-matched observed movement endpoints and source-generated available destination habitat-category counts for greater white-fronted geese, lesser snow geese, mallards and northern pintails. Seven source-defined categories include protected/refuge habitat as well as land-cover types. The Louisiana extension compared consecutive observed habitat categories with expectations from source-matched available categories and an individual-year-specific time-invariant preference distribution. Its separate, explicitly post-result test examined whether scarcity of the previously used category among currently available destinations corresponded to a switch, within source bird-year, previous category, winter season and photoperiod. Candidate biological-bird IDs were used to account for repeated years; final source-curator verification of the IDs remains pending.

**Additional ecological comparison of diel habitat-state succession.** We used the same source-validated 329 bird-years, of which 9,586 successive, approximately hourly endpoint pairs crossed a source-defined day/night photoperiod boundary and 46,947 remained within a photoperiod; 58 more crossed winter-season labels and were excluded. For each source individual bird-year, a fixed marginal habitat-choice reference was fitted to its seven class uses and time-varying matched available destinations. We compared the remaining observed category switching at day/night boundaries with same-photoperiod transitions, within bird-year, season, arrival photoperiod and previously used habitat category, before candidate individual-level uncertainty evaluation. We then reported a further explicitly post-result category-specific decomposition of successive Protected/Rice observations at day-to-night versus night-to-day transitions. Neither analysis identifies the actual habitat at the unrecorded start of the moving step or the function of a management-class Protected destination.

### Ecological comparison and inferential separation

The units of evidence intentionally differ. Species-by-time organization at camera sites, held-out individual altitude-use shapes, the variance and coupling of water depths used by marked birds, and categorical habitat persistence cannot be treated as repeated measurements of one common biological effect. We therefore compare **the direction and ecological meaning of each independently evaluated contrast** instead of combining effect sizes, confidence intervals or p values. The four California waterfowl species belong to one monitoring programme, not four independently collected experiments.

We distinguish three kinds of inference throughout the Results: a demonstrated pattern in an individual dataset; an ecological explanation compatible with that pattern; and an untested cross-system hypothesis. We do not infer temporal competition from time partitioning alone, foraging or fitness benefits from bat altitude individuality, or adaptive movement from water-depth buffering alone.

## Results

### Species visiting the same savanna sites differed in detection-time organization

In the ODSP analysis of the Serengeti camera survey, 17 admitted mammal species occupied detection-time states that were not independent of species identity within the same camera sites. Conditional species–time information was 0.224 nats (permutation p = 0.005). The temporal dimension contained approximately 5.15 effective detection-time states after site was accounted for. Independent held-out site-fold log-score gains were +0.0572, +0.0452 and +0.0451, each positive.

The ecological result is within-site differentiation in the timing of recorded use, which remains detectable beyond the source spatial location. Such differentiation can create different encounter schedules among taxa sharing sites. It does not by itself show that one species avoids another, that all taxa are exclusively nocturnal or diurnal, or that the camera record perfectly represents underlying activity rhythms.

### Bats within the same broad horizontal habitat were not necessarily vertically interchangeable

The earlier ODSP case study of *T. teniotis* found approximately 4.02 effective vertical states within supported 5-km horizontal cells, but the population-trained vertical structure failed to improve prediction for either of two withheld individuals (log-score gains −0.435 and −0.022). The observation that a horizontal cell holds several used altitudinal states therefore did not imply a transferable pooled vertical-use profile.

The subsequent batter comparison yielded stronger positive biological evidence for individuality in the distribution of vertical use. After equalizing coarse horizontal occupancy and subtracting the session-specific median altitude, repeatable individual identity remained supported in five of six panels: *E. helvum*, *H. monstrosus* and three *P. hastatus* year panels. Calibrated excess identity scores were +0.443, +0.177, +0.111, +0.118 and +0.574, respectively, with positive panel-specific tests. The motivating *T. teniotis* panel was the required exception (calibrated excess −0.022, p = 0.512).

Thus several bat populations contained individuals that differed consistently in how their vertical use was distributed around their session-typical altitude, not merely in their absolute mean height or coarse horizontal footprint. The distinct vertical-use shapes may reflect differences in flight behaviour, allocation among feeding and commuting states, or finer spatial preferences, but none of those processes was separated causally in these tracking sources. The failure of *Tadarida* after median centring prevents the result from being described as a universal individual specialization across the six panels.

### Resident King Rails maintained a narrower experienced water-depth state than the local sampled alternatives

In the western Lake Erie King Rail series, the observed use of water depth varied less than contemporaneous local pseudo-use in all 10 birds. The median individual state-retention index was 0.886, and an independently evaluated temporal successive-change retention index had a median of 0.658, also positive in all 10 birds. Coupling of used water depth to local available depth had a slope of 0.162, compared with approximately 1.000 under matched pseudo-use: an 83.8% reduction in this observational coupling.

The biological distinction is that temporal variation in the wetland's sampled accessible depths did not translate proportionally into variation in the water depth actually used. This shows the potential importance of selective microhabitat use for realized environmental exposure. In a post-hoc comparison of 173 events from the same birds, broader local contrast between the two sampled depth alternatives corresponded to a slightly smaller deviation from each bird's typical used depth (heterogeneity slope −0.043 versus +0.262 under pseudo-use; Monte Carlo p = 0.000020). A stronger prediction that heterogeneity becomes disproportionately more protective during extreme mismatches was not supported.

In a second, clearly exploratory chronological comparison within this same source, the birds returned to the typical depth range defined from their earlier observations (individual median ±6 cm) on 56.7% of later observations, compared with 21.0% of the matched two-point local reference sample. This supports within-individual persistence of preferred measured depths over the source observation period. It neither demonstrates physiological depth targets nor shows that particular geographical relocations caused stabilization.

### Waterfowl habitat-state continuity was widespread, but opportunity-dependent switching was not uniform

The separate California tracking analysis extended temporal continuity from measured water depth to categorical destinations of movement steps. Across 329 bird-years, assigned to 254 candidate stable tagged-bird identities, all four species showed observed same-category continuation exceeding the expectation from independent choices with each bird-year's fixed marginal habitat preference. Equal-individual excess continuation was +13.95 percentage points in greater white-fronted geese (95% bird-cluster interval +12.52 to +15.40), +15.87 in lesser snow geese (+14.88 to +16.87), +6.55 in mallards (+2.22 to +10.66) and +11.88 in northern pintails (+10.17 to +13.64).

This widespread continuation was not equivalent to unchanging habitat occupancy. In a distinct post-result analysis restricted to 46,947 within-season and within-photoperiod successive pairs, a 10-percentage-point reduction in the available share of the previously occupied habitat class corresponded to an approximately +5.29-point change in the probability of switching among greater white-fronted geese, +5.01 in lesser snow geese and +5.06 in northern pintails. Their individual-cluster intervals were positive. The mallard estimate was small and uncertain (+0.61 points, 95% interval −4.20 to +4.14). The actual time alignment exceeded four predetermined season- and photoperiod-preserving shifts in the first three species but not in mallards.

The joint ecological picture within this source is therefore **state persistence together with conditional replacement**: birds frequently returned to the same type of habitat, while the current composition of feasible destination types was associated with departures from that continuity in three of four species. The published source describes mallards as relatively generalist in habitat selection and geese and northern pintails as more similar for some resource-related responses (Overton and Casazza 2023). This offers a biologically plausible candidate explanation for differences in our temporal switching associations, but the limited number of mallard individuals and a single California study do not establish a general specialist–generalist law.

### Diel habitat switching had a species-specific boundary structure

The newly evaluated California source retained 9,586 day/night boundary pairs from the same 329 bird-years. Relative to within-photoperiod hourly pairs, and after comparing within the same source season, arrival photoperiod and previously observed habitat category, lesser snow geese displayed **+8.47 percentage points** of additional category switching beyond matched availability and bird-year constant habitat choice (candidate-ID 95% CI +5.72 to +11.16; 69 estimable marked IDs; 54 with positive candidate-individual contrasts; Figure 3). This association was not clearly detected in greater white-fronted geese (+0.55, −3.29 to +4.26, 44 IDs), mallards (+3.14, −11.94 to +18.22, five IDs) or northern pintails (+1.53, −4.35 to +7.41, 28 IDs). Thus greater general activity around twilight is not sufficient as an explanation for a uniform habitat-category switching response across species.

A particularly interpretable difference concerned **the next habitat category after Protected-category use**. In lesser snow geese, an observed Protected endpoint was followed by a Rice endpoint 191 times in source day-to-night transitions and 53 times in night-to-day transitions. After subtracting the fitted bird-year fixed-choice expectation under current available categories, the equal-candidate-ID dusk-minus-dawn difference was **+32.36 percentage points** (95% CI +22.13 to +43.33; 47 estimable IDs). Northern pintails showed the same directional pattern (49 versus 10 source successions; adjusted +55.54 points, +37.53 to +72.77; 18 IDs), while greater white-fronted geese had an uncertain adjusted contrast (+9.70, −1.41 to +21.26; 11 IDs) and mallards lacked the required independent sample. The reciprocal Rice-then-Protected succession showed no equally clear day-to-night excess in the sufficiently sampled species (Figure 4).

These observations are about **consecutive recorded moving-step destinations**, not verified continuous routes from protected land to rice fields. Protected describes management status; Rice describes land cover. Their biological roles as daytime refuge and nocturnal feeding habitat remain a plausible but untested explanation. The destination-specific analysis was chosen after examining descriptive direction counts, making it explicitly exploratory rather than an independent mechanistic confirmation. The original source paper already described between-species circadian movement and habitat selection; what is newly evaluated here is the *direction and timing of change in successively observed habitat states*.

## Discussion

### Habitat overlap conceals different ecological histories

Taken together, these source-resolved results support an ecological distinction that a two-dimensional map cannot show. The same site can be part of different species' detection-time schedules; the same broad horizontal region can be occupied by bats with distinct individual vertical-use distributions; and the same wetland can contain a wider range of available water depths than any one rail typically experiences. In each case, the ecological meaning of shared space changes when use is resolved by biological identity or environmental state.

These are not four independent tests of an identical theoretical effect. Rather, they illustrate different levels of niche organization: **species-specific timing, individual vertical use, and the temporal trajectory of habitats actually experienced**. The ecological value of the comparison lies in identifying these levels and their different possible consequences, not in compressing their statistics into one generic index.

### Stable use and responsive switching can coexist

The California observations show why fidelity and flexibility are not necessarily opposing strategies. All four species had substantial excess continuation of habitat category, yet current opportunities were associated with switching in three species. A bird can maintain repeated use of a resource class over many hours while changing category when the local set of destinations changes. This is a condition-dependent pattern rather than a permanent individual identity label.

The distinction also clarifies the King Rail result. A narrow experienced-depth trajectory does not require that every point of the wetland remain hydrologically constant. It is compatible with animals using changing spatial mosaics of available depths, but the source does not identify each step's actual movement. Persistence can reflect staying within a stable patch, selecting similar depths elsewhere, or a combination. The observed reduction in water-depth coupling is therefore a real ecological pattern with more than one possible behavioural mechanism.

### The ecological role of heterogeneity depends on its organization and accessibility

Environmental heterogeneity can present organisms with a portfolio of microhabitats, but the presence of alternatives on a map is not enough to establish their functional availability. A water-depth class must be encountered within the actual movement range and under the individual's behavioural constraints; a vertical stratum must be accessible and relevant to the bat's behaviour; a wetland cover type must be represented among movements possible from the current location. Heterogeneity therefore has ecological consequences through *available choice sets and the sequences of choices made*, not as an automatic function of the number of categories or altitude bins.

The Lake Erie association between local depth contrast and smaller deviations from typical use is compatible with the idea that fine-scale wetland heterogeneity provides local alternatives. It should not be converted into proof that increased heterogeneity causally prevented displacement: the two available reference points per event are a sparse sample. Indeed, a later post-hoc model using the proportion of only two random plots inside an individual's previously used depth band did not provide robust added held-out predictive value beyond mean-depth mismatch and range. This failed proxy does not refute the ecological value of genuinely accessible alternatives; it constrains what the archived sampling design can identify.

### Species-specific ecological circuits within shared agricultural wetlands

The new California contrasts show why even taxa using similar landscapes need not follow the same daily habitat-state sequence. A positive general twilight switching excess appeared in lesser snow geese, but not convincingly in greater white-fronted geese or northern pintails. Snow geese and pintails nevertheless shared a more specific directional succession: after recorded use of protected lands, rice landscapes disproportionately followed at the onset of night, relative to the reverse source photoperiod boundary and fixed habitat preference. This adds temporal organization to the original publication's static habitat selection and species functional responses.

One ecological interpretation is a daily circuit linking land managed for refuge and the broader agricultural resource mosaic. The source does not indicate whether an individual was feeding, resting or escaping hunting pressure, and category-change signals may equally reflect location-dependent movement opportunities or differences in which steps are captured around dawn and dusk. In particular, the previous *endpoint* is not a source-observed habitat at the true start of the next movement, so it would be unjustified to call these data direct refuge-to-feeding flight trajectories. The directional result offers a biologically explicit hypothesis for animal-borne tracking and observational behavioural studies.

Importantly, these newly resolved within-individual time sequences do not imply that individual birds were maintaining their water depth, as in the distinct King Rail observation. The Serengeti camera detections likewise concern between-species timing at fixed sites, not directed habitat movement. Their common contribution is to reveal that shared geographical distribution can conceal **different ecological schedules of state use**, from species timing to individual resource-category succession.

### Ecological differences and testable explanations

The systems suggest several biological explanations worth distinguishing in future field studies. Temporal differentiation at shared sites may arise from intrinsic diel rhythms, thermal stress, prey behaviour, predation risk or competition; the Serengeti source alone does not partition these causes. Individual altitude-use shapes may reflect repeated feeding versus commuting strategies, use of canopy or airspace structure, route fidelity or individual morphology; the bat records do not independently measure the fitness payoffs. Stability of experienced water depth may reflect active movement, spatial persistence in stable patches, or persistent physiological and behavioural preferences. California's stronger opportunity–switch association in geese and northern pintails, compared with an uncertain mallard estimate, suggests a potential relation to resource specialization that requires replication with adequate within-species variation in resource breadth.

Three specific ecological predictions follow. First, the strength of individual state-use differences should persist when animals are compared in the same season, behavioural state and physically accessible habitat set; disappearance of differences after those controls would favor behavioural or spatial composition rather than independent individual strategy. Second, actual experienced-state stability should remain larger than predicted from spatially constrained movement and fixed patch residence alone if active state maintenance contributes. Third, in environments where a frequently used state becomes less accessible, state switching should increase before or alongside broader relocation, but the response may differ between resource specialists and generalists.

### Consequences for biodiversity assessment and conservation

A habitat map that records only occupancy can miss how organisms divide opportunities within that habitat. For biodiversity monitoring, time-resolved camera detections can help identify different site-use schedules, while altitude-resolved tracking can reveal individual-use heterogeneity concealed by horizontal overlap. In fluctuating wetlands, environmental conditions at occupied sites and the conditions individuals actually use should both be monitored. Habitats that offer multiple reachable shallow and deep microhabitats may plausibly provide opportunities to maintain acceptable states as water levels vary.

These are monitoring and management hypotheses, not tested treatment effects. The California results do not establish the benefit of specific refuge or flood-management actions, and the King Rail study does not quantify survival, reproductive success or restored hydrological buffering. The defensible ecological recommendation is to avoid equating an unchanged occurrence map with unchanged ecological exposure, and to measure availability and realized use at biologically meaningful times and state dimensions.

### Limits of the comparative evidence

All four programmes are observational secondary analyses; their comparison was chosen after source outcomes were known. They differ in study area, species composition, time resolution, environmental axes, sample units and observational biases. ODSP's *Tadarida* use-support case overlaps the focal batter observations. The California four species derive from a single programme, and the candidate repeated-ID structure needs final source-curator authentication. The bat case's individual-shape result cannot identify foraging success, and camera site-by-time partitioning cannot identify competitive exclusion. Neither wetland programme independently reconstructs the hydrological condition the animal would have experienced by staying at the exact old geographical location at the next observation time.

Accordingly, this manuscript establishes **an ecological synthesis of supported state-use patterns**, not an independently pooled empirical estimate of one cross-taxon effect or a proven causal trajectory from niche thickness to individual specialization to environmental buffering. That remaining trajectory is a hypothesis motivated by the differences as much as by the positive results.

## Conclusion

The biological use of a shared place is not a single ecological state. Across these public observational sources, mammals visiting the same camera sites differed in detection timing, several bat populations contained individually repeatable shapes of vertical use, King Rails maintained comparatively stable experienced water depths, and wintering waterfowl displayed both categorical habitat persistence and opportunity-associated switching, with additional nightfall-related Protected/Rice category succession in lesser snow geese and northern pintails. The common ecological lesson is that **habitat use is organized by biological identity and by time**, so similar mapped occupancy need not mean similar environmental experience. Identifying when that organization becomes an adaptive buffer will require independent environmental measurements at used and unused locations, actual movement constraints, and fitness or demographic consequences. Until then, the positive biological patterns—and their species-specific exceptions—stand as the substantive ecological findings.
